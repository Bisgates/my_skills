# Chapter 17: Progress — The Power of Small Wins

## Core Idea
Of all forms of human motivation the most effective is progress, so the Essentialist starts small, builds momentum, and celebrates small wins in essential areas instead of launching big flashy initiatives that flare out.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Minimal viable progress**: adaptation of the startup "minimal viable product" to execution.
  - When to use: starting any essential task where the temptation is to over-scope.
  - How: ask "What is the smallest amount of progress that will be useful and valuable to the essential task we are trying to get done?" — then ship that, gather signal, iterate.
  - Why it works / failure mode: each small concrete win creates momentum and affirms faith in further success, so the eventual breakthrough looks like overnight success; the caveat is it's not license to "produce rubbish" — the increment must still be useful and valuable.
- **Minimal viable preparation**: "start early and small" instead of "late and big."
  - When to use: any important goal or deadline weeks or months out.
  - How: at the earliest possible moment, invest the minimal possible time — ask "What is the minimal amount I could do right now to prepare?" Concrete doses: ten minutes on a project two weeks before due; open a file and spend four minutes (no more) on a future presentation; fifteen seconds typing meeting objectives at scheduling time.
- **Visually reward progress**: make advancement visible (fund-raiser thermometer, star chart) so the behavior reinforces itself without prodding.
  - When to use: any essential goal at home or work where motivation needs to survive weeks.
  - How: pick a visible progress display, update it on every win, don't be "above" the kindergarten technique.
- **Design systems that make good behavior easy** (Positive Tickets pattern): instead of punishing failure after the fact, catch and reward the desired behavior until it becomes natural and effortless.

## Key Concepts
- **Small wins**: small, simple victories in essential areas — the Essentialist's alternative to big flashy wins that don't matter.
- **Progress principle** (Amabile & Kramer, diary study of hundreds of workers): "of all the things that can boost emotions, motivation, and perceptions during a workday, the single most important is making progress in meaningful work."
- **Herzberg's two internal motivators** (1968 HBR): achievement and recognition for achievement.
- **Positive Tickets**: Richmond, Canada policing program rewarding youth caught doing good (redeemable for movies/youth-center entry); ~40,000 positive interactions/year for a decade cut recidivism from 60% to 8% (vs. the 65% baseline under traditional enforcement).
- **"Done is better than perfect"**: Silicon Valley maxim — not an excuse for rubbish, but a directive to stop spending on nonessentials and finish.
- **"Early and small" vs. "late and big"**: the two opposing approaches to deadlines; all-nighter heroics vs. minimal early investment.
- **Conditioning momentum** (Zimbardo): the Stanford prison experiment shows treatment shapes behavior within days; the Heroic Imagination Project applies the same conditioning toward positive behavior.

## Mental Models
- Think of motivation as compounding from progress: use the next small win to fund the one after, rather than spending everything on a launch.
- Use system design instead of willpower-policing when a behavior keeps requiring enforcement — change the incentive structure once, then let it run.
- Think of "the more we reach for the stars, the harder it is to get ourselves off the ground": ambition expressed as scope is anti-progress.

## Anti-patterns
- **Going big on everything**: the Nonessentialist starts with a big goal and gets small results; hyped initiatives flare out with nothing to show but wasted time and energy (the executive who announced an elaborate dollhouse for his daughters, then abandoned it as too burdensome).
- **Doing more of what already fails, more forcefully**: harsher laws / zero tolerance as the default crime response — reactive, negative, after the fact.
- **Manual policing of behavior**: parents consciously enforcing screen limits creates constant friction and consumes the parents' essential time.

## Worked Example
The McKeown family token system: screen time had crept up; turning off the TV produced complaints and constant parental policing. Redesign: each child gets 10 tokens per week; each token = 30 minutes of screen time or 50 cents (cap: 5 hours or $5/week); reading a book for 30 minutes earns one extra token, tradable the same way. Result overnight: screen time down 90%, reading up by the same amount, policing effort collapsed. One small setup investment, then the system ran without friction — nonessential activity down, essential activity up.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask "What is the smallest amount of progress that will be useful and valuable to the essential task?" before scoping anything.
2. Start preparation absurdly early and absurdly small: four minutes on the deck months ahead, fifteen seconds of objectives when booking a meeting.
3. Test ideas with the cheapest possible probe before committing (McKeown: tweet the idea → if it resonates, HBR blog post → book chapter; Pixar: storyboards iterated hundreds of times before scripting).
4. Make progress visible — thermometers and star charts work on adults.
5. When you want behavior change in others, build a reward system for the desired behavior instead of punishing the undesired one.
