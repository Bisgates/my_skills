# Chapter 4: Trade-off — Which Problem Do I Want?

## Core Idea
"We can try to avoid the reality of trade-offs, but we can't escape them": saying yes to any opportunity by definition requires saying no to several others, so the only question is whether the sacrifice happens by design or by default at the margins.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Which problem do I want?"**: the Essentialist's replacement question for the Nonessentialist's "How can I do both?"
  - When to use: any decision where both options are genuinely wanted — more pay vs. more vacation, finish the email vs. be on time, faster vs. better.
  - How: (1) refuse the "I can do both" frame; (2) state both losses explicitly; (3) pick which problem you would rather live with; (4) reframe positively — instead of "What do I have to give up?" ask "What do I want to go big on?"
  - Why it works / failure mode: a trade-off by definition involves two things we want, so "yes to both" always feels preferable — but the I-can-do-both logic is false, and refusing the choice doesn't avoid the loss; it just relocates it to wherever you least control it (late to the meeting, shoddy on both deadlines).
- **Straddling (Porter)**: keeping your existing strategy intact while simultaneously trying to adopt a competitor's strategy.
  - When to use: as a diagnostic for organizations or people trying to run two incompatible strategies at once.
  - How it fails: Continental Lite copied Southwest's surface practices (lower fares, no meals, no first class, frequent departures) while retaining the legacy business model, so it lacked the operational efficiencies to compete on price and was forced to skimp at the margins — hundreds of millions lost, "a thousand complaints a day," CEO fired. Porter: "A strategic position is not sustainable unless there are trade-offs with other positions."
- **Priority-ordered values (the J&J Credo)**: a value statement that ranks constituents instead of listing virtues.
  - When to use: writing any mission/values document, or any personal criteria list, that must guide decisions under conflict.
  - How: list whom/what you serve in explicit priority order (the 1943 Credo puts customers first, shareholders last); when the 1982 Tylenol cyanide murders forced a choice between customer safety and ~$100 million, the ranking made the recall decision clear. Contrast: "We value passion, innovation, execution, and leadership" — who doesn't? — says nothing about what to choose when values collide.

## Key Concepts
- **Trade-off**: a forced choice between two things you want; per Thomas Sowell, "There are no solutions. There are only trade-offs."
- **By design vs. by default**: deliberate strategic sacrifice (Southwest) versus marginal sacrifice forced on you by refusing to choose (Continental, Erin Callan).
- **Straddled strategy**: attempting two incompatible strategies simultaneously; unsustainable because it makes no trade-offs.
- **Boundary creep**: Erin Callan (former Lehman CFO) — a half-hour of Sunday email became a few hours, then all day; "slight modifications became the new normal... until work was all that was left." Trade-offs made by default, one increment at a time.
- **"Go big" framing**: the Essentialist treats trade-offs as an inherent part of life, not an inherently negative one — an opportunity to strategically select, not a loss to mourn.
- **The four-burner stove**: family, friends, health, work — "to be successful you have to cut off one of your burners; to be really successful you have to cut off two." McKeown cites it tongue-in-cheek: not a prescription, but a reminder to ask "Which problem do you want?" when options prioritize different burners.

## Mental Models
- Use Southwest as the template for deliberate trade-offs: point-to-point only, no meals, no assigned seats, coach only — every cut serving one clear identity (low-cost airline), accepted risk of alienating customers who want more. Kelleher: "We're not going to do a thousand different things that really won't contribute much to the end result we are trying to achieve."
- Think of an unranked values list as no values list — guidance only exists where the ordering resolves conflicts.
- When tempted by "I can fit one more thing in," recall that the cost doesn't vanish; it gets paid at the margin you didn't pick.
- Treat creeping commitments like Callan's Sundays: the dangerous trade-offs are the ones made in increments too small to trigger a decision.

## Anti-patterns
- **"I can do both" / "We can do it all"**: the executive who cut her eighteen "top priority" IT projects to seventeen when asked for five — spreading five projects' worth of resources across seventeen and getting none of the results.
- **Vanilla value statements**: "passion, innovation, execution, leadership" — tells employees nothing about what the company values *most* or how to decide when values are at odds; same flaw as "we serve all stakeholders equally."
- **Imitating a strategy without its trade-offs**: copying the visible practices (Continental Lite) while keeping the old model intact.
- **Avoiding trade-offs to avoid pain**: the sacrifice still happens — by default, at the margins, on terms you didn't choose.

## Worked Example
Southwest vs. Continental Lite, end to end. 1972–2002: a dollar in each S&P 500 company — best return is not GE, IBM, or Intel but Southwest Airlines, in an industry notorious for losing money. Kelleher's design: only point-to-point routes, no meals, no advance seat assignment, coach only — each cut deliberate, all serving the low-cost identity, accepting the alienation risk. Critics lambast it; profits soar. Continental responds by straddling: Continental Lite copies the visible cuts but keeps the legacy model on most flights, so it can't reach the cost structure that makes low fares viable, skimps incoherently at the margins, loses hundreds of millions, generates a thousand complaints a day, and the CEO is fired. Same industry, same decade, same practices available — the difference is whether trade-offs were made by design or by default.

## Key Takeaways
1. Replace "How can I do both?" with "Which problem do I want?" — name the loss you're choosing to accept.
2. Reframe again to the offensive version: "What do I want to go big on?"
3. Rank your values/constituents in priority order *before* the crisis; an unranked list decides nothing (J&J Credo vs. the $40B CEO's list).
4. Never copy a strategy without copying its trade-offs; straddling is the expensive middle.
5. Audit for boundary creep: trade-offs made in slight, repeated modifications are still trade-offs — Callan's Sundays.
6. Cutting a list of 18 to 17 is refusing the exercise; forcing it to 5 is where the value is.
