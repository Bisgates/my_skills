# Chapter 19: Focus — What's Important Now?

## Core Idea
Execution becomes effortless when you tune in fully to the present: you can only ever act in the now, so every second spent replaying the past or worrying about the future is subtracted from the one place contribution actually happens.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **W.I.N. — "What's important now?"** (Larry Gelwix, Highland rugby: 418–10, twenty national championships in thirty-six years).
  - When to use: mid-execution whenever attention drifts to the last mistake, the future outcome, or the competition.
  - How: (1) apply the question constantly during play — not next week's match, not the next play, this one; (2) keep focus on *your own* game, not the opponent's — watching the other team divides the group and drags you into playing their way; unity around one strategy makes execution frictionless.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Gelwix distinguishes being beaten (they were better) from losing (you lost focus on what was essential) — the framework controls the second, which is the only one you control. Fails when the question gets aimed at tomorrow ("what's important next?") instead of this very second.
- **Triage-the-now procedure** (McKeown's hotel-room reset):
  - When to use: overwhelmed, many tasks vying for top billing simultaneously.
  - How: (1) stop; breathe; ask "What's important now?" — if unclear, *figuring that out* is what's important now; (2) clear the physical field — tidy objects away, phone off; (3) write a list of everything on your mind; (4) ask "What do you need to do to be able to go to sleep peacefully?" and cross off everything not important right now; (5) **get the future out of your head** — on a separate page list "What might you want to do someday as a result of today?" (ideas, not commitments) so nothing is forgotten and nothing demands action this second; (6) prioritize both lists and work the now-list one item at a time, erasing on completion.
- **"The pause that refreshes"** (Jeffrey A. Rodgers): a transition ritual — at the front door, stop, close eyes, one slow deep breath; on the exhale let the work issues fall away, then enter with singleness of purpose.

## Key Concepts
- **Chronos**: the Greeks' quantitative clock-time — measured, raced through, used "efficiently."
- **Kairos**: qualitative, opportune time — experienced only when fully in the moment.
- **Multitasking vs. multifocusing**: doing two things at once is fine (dishes + radio); *concentrating* on two things at once is impossible — the enemy is pretending to multifocus, not multitasking itself.
- **Losing vs. being beaten**: being beaten means they were faster, stronger, more talented; losing means you lost focus on the essential.
- **Beginner's mind / mindfulness** (Thich Nhat Hanh): "Mindfulness helps you go home to the present" — kairos taught under another name.
- **Kairos journal**: record your kairos moments daily, note what triggered each and what broke it, then deliberately re-create the triggers.
- **Execution paradox**: "execution is easy if you work hard at it and hard if you work easy at it" — full presence is the hard work that makes it easy.

## Mental Models
- Use W.I.N. as an interrupt handler: whenever you catch the replay loop (past) or the worry loop (future), ask what is important this very second.
- Think of attention like Gelwix's rugby team: watching the opponent's game means playing their game; focus on your own.
- Treat a parked "someday" list as memory you've paged out: written down, ideas stop demanding execution now.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mind split across competing subjects**: present in body, absent in mind — the Stanford classmate who, while asking McKeown for job help, texted for two full minutes mid-conversation and lost the recommendation he was about to get.
- **Endless replay / future worry**: rehashing past mistakes and stressing over what's ahead spends energy on what cannot be controlled, at the cost of what can.
- **Pretending to multifocus**: trying to concentrate on two things at once degrades both; do, don't concentrate, in parallel.

## Worked Example
McKeown returns to his hotel room after teaching all day; everything in the room demands action (e-mail, messages, an obligatory book, a future presentation, the day's ideas). Anxiety rises. He stops, kneels, asks "What's important now?" — realizes the first task is determining that. He tidies the room, turns off the phone, journals about the day, lists everything on his mind, then asks what he needs to do to sleep peacefully: connect with his wife and children, then only the items that make tomorrow's first hours effortless (book wake-up call and breakfast, load slides, iron shirt). A second journal page catches "someday" ideas. He works the now-list calmly, one item at a time — finishing everything better and faster because he was focused.

## Key Takeaways
1. When overwhelmed, the first essential task is to figure out what is important now — stop, breathe, list, cross off.
2. Maintain two lists: "essential now" (execute one at a time) and "someday as a result of today" (ideas parked, not commitments).
3. Remove ambient demand: tidy the objects that pressure you and put a barrier between yourself and incoming texts.
4. Build transition rituals between contexts — one deliberate breath at the door buys presence with your family.
5. Multitask freely; never attempt to multifocus.
6. Log kairos moments, identify their triggers, and engineer their recurrence.
