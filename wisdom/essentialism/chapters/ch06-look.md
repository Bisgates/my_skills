# Chapter 6: Look — See What Really Matters

## Core Idea
In every set of facts something essential is hidden; the skill is not gathering information but finding "the lead" — the point that makes everything else cohere. Be a journalist of your own life: filter for signal, not volume.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Find the Lead**: A lead is not a summary of who/what/when/where — it is what the facts *mean* and why they matter (Simms's journalism exercise: the lead isn't "faculty travels to Sacramento Thursday," it's "There will be no school Thursday").
  - When to use: whenever you face a pile of facts, requests, or data and feel unsure what to focus on.
  - How: (1) lay out the facts; (2) ask what they mean for the people affected, not what they say; (3) make the relationships between pieces explicit; (4) state the point in one sentence.
  - Why it works / failure mode: summarizing feels like understanding but isn't — every student "correctly" compressed the facts and still missed the point. The failure mode is hyperfocus on a true-but-minor detail (Flight 401: crew fixated on a burnt-out gear indicator light while the autopilot was off and the plane descended into the Everglades).
- **Filter for the Fascinating** (Thomas Friedman's method): take in the whole conversation but explore only what genuinely grabs your attention; ask questions only about that.
  - When to use: meetings, reading, any high-volume information stream.
  - How: listen for what is said only at the periphery and for what is *not* being said; read between the lines; then connect the dots with targeted questions.
- **Keep a Journal — "less but better" edition**: write *less than you feel like writing* daily; review entries every ~90 days for patterns, not details.
  - When to use: building self-observation without burning out on day-3 abandonment.
  - How: restrain entry length until daily journaling is a habit; in the quarterly hour-long review, skip individual events and capture the headline of your week/quarter — small incremental changes are invisible day-to-day but huge cumulatively.
- **Clarify the Question**: when a discussion sprawls, stop and ask "What question are we trying to answer?" — repeat until the group can actually state it.
  - When to use: meetings or solo work that has devolved into opinions spawning opinions.
  - How: halt; force an explicit statement of the goal and the decisions needed; listen for hidden themes connecting the scattered ideas; only then assign actions.

## Key Concepts
- **The lead**: the essential meaning of a story — why it matters — as distinct from its facts.
- **Signal in the noise**: what the Essentialist listens for; the Nonessentialist hears the loudest voice and gets the wrong message.
- **What is not being said**: the periphery and the omissions, often where the essence hides.
- **The spiderweb of the story** (Mariam Semaan): deep contextual knowledge of a topic that lets you spot the abnormal detail that doesn't fit the natural course of events.
- **Role play**: putting yourself in each main player's shoes to understand motives and perspectives — a route to a fresh angle.
- **Getting into the field**: firsthand observation of the real context before solving (Embrace incubator team in Nepal).
- **Fire extinguishers in times of flood** (C. S. Lewis): reacting energetically to the wrong problem.

## Mental Models
- Think of a journal as a backup drive for your brain's faulty memory — "the faintest pencil is better than the strongest memory."
- Use the journalist's stance when overwhelmed by requests: don't relay information, discover what matters; connect dots into trends instead of reacting to dots.
- Use the Flight 401 check when deep in a problem: am I debugging the indicator light while the plane loses altitude?

## Anti-patterns
- **Listening while preparing to reply**: you hear everything and miss the point.
- **Hyperfocusing on inconsequential detail**: technically diligent, catastrophically misallocated attention.
- **Racing to solve the stated problem**: the d.school team would have built a cheap *electric* incubator; field observation revealed 80% of the babies were born in homes without electricity — the real constraint was "no electricity at all."
- **Evading the hard question with vague answers**: starts a spiral of further vagueness and misinformation.

## Worked Example
Salesforce SVP Elay Cohen's six-person team in a timed business simulation: every proposed answer spawned more opinions; fifteen minutes in, a straightforward exercise had become a sprawling debate. McKeown (observing) asked "What question are you trying to answer?" — awkward silence, then another tangent. He asked again, and again, until the team stopped, stated their actual goal and the decisions required, filtered the scattered ideas for connecting themes, made the decisions, and divided responsibilities. They went from "motion sickness to momentum" and won by a landslide. The intervention was nothing but forcing the question to be explicit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before processing facts, ask what they mean and to whom — write the one-sentence lead.
2. In any meeting or stream, deliberately listen for what is not being said.
3. Journal daily but write less than you want to; review every ~90 days for trends only.
4. Go see the real context firsthand before committing to a solution framing.
5. When discussion sprawls, halt and demand "what question are we answering?" — repeat until answered.
6. Build domain knowledge so abnormal details stand out against the story's spiderweb.
