# Chapter 16: Subtract — Bring Forth More by Removing Obstacles

## Core Idea
An Essentialist improves output not by adding effort but by finding the single constraint — the "slowest hiker" — holding the whole system back, and making a one-time investment in removing it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Theory of Constraints / "slowest hiker"** (from Goldratt's *The Goal*): the system moves only as fast as its slowest element; improving anything other than the constraint produces no material improvement.
  - When to use: any stalled project, process, or routine where added effort isn't yielding results.
  - How: (1) locate the constraint (in a plant: the machine with the biggest queue of material behind it; in a hike: the boy miles behind); (2) put it at the front — make the whole system pace around it; (3) lighten its load directly (Alex takes weight out of Herbie's backpack); (4) once it speeds up, the next-slowest element becomes the new constraint — repeat one at a time.
  - Why it works / failure mode: any improvement to the constraint instantly improves the whole system; improvements anywhere else are invisible. Fails when applied haphazardly — fixing "things that need fixing here and there" yields marginal gains at best and wasted effort at worst.
- **Three-step obstacle removal**: McKeown's procedure for executing one essential outcome.
  - When to use: a concrete deliverable is stuck or about to start.
  - How: (1) **Be clear about the essential intent** — ask "How will we know when we are done?" and define a deliberately precise outcome (e.g., "15-page draft report attached to an e-mail, sent to the client by 2:00 P.M. Thursday"); (2) **Identify the slowest hiker** — list every obstacle, then prioritize with "What is the obstacle that, if removed, would make the majority of other obstacles disappear?"; (3) **Remove it** — one obstacle at a time, primary first.

## Key Concepts
- **Constraint**: the obstacle holding the whole system back; improving everything else while ignoring it yields no material improvement.
- **Poiesis** (Aristotle via Heidegger): the third kind of work beyond theoretical (truth) and practical (action) — a "bringing-forth"; the Essentialist mode of execution.
- **Essential intent (precise form)**: a done-condition specific enough that you know exactly when you're finished; without it "all change is arbitrary."
- **"Productive" obstacles**: activities that feel like work — research, e-mailing for information, perfectionist rewriting — but slow the actual goal; anything slowing the stated outcome should be questioned.
- **One priority at a time**: removing arbitrary obstacles has zero effect while the primary one doesn't budge (hiring a researcher doesn't help if getting words on the page is the constraint).
- **"Catch more flies with honey"**: when the slowest hiker is a person (boss, finance, client), go see them and ask "What obstacles or bottlenecks are holding you back from achieving X, and how can I help remove these?" instead of sending another demand.
- **Boulder at the top of a hill**: obstacle removal needs only a small initial shove; momentum builds naturally.

## Mental Models
- Think of execution as subtraction, not addition: instead of "what resources do we add?" (more salespeople, more production), ask "what constraint do we remove?"
- Use the queue test when diagnosing a process: the constraint is wherever the biggest backlog piles up behind.
- Think of pressure as friction-generating: the harder you push on a person, the harder they push back; remove their obstacles instead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Quick-fix / Band-Aid solutions**: the Nonessentialist reacts to crises with finger-in-the-dam patches; pressure piles up, friction grows, nothing structural improves.
- **Hammer thinking**: being good with a hammer, treating everything as a nail — applying more force where the problem is a constraint, not a lack of effort.
- **Fixing everything at once**: parallel obstacle removal dilutes the only intervention that matters.

## Worked Example
The author's wife, with three small children, is strained and unable to make her highest contribution. Applying the steps: the essential intent is full engagement with the children; the listed obstacles reduce to one primary constraint — no uninterrupted time to plan, think, and prepare. Removal: he opts out of evening extracurriculars; they hire a few hours of weekly childcare. Result: both parents do less in total but are fully present — "less, but better" achieved by removing one obstacle, not adding effort.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before any project, spend a few minutes listing obstacles and ask which one, if removed, makes most of the others disappear.
2. Define the done-condition with deliberate precision (deliverable, format, recipient, deadline) before touching the work.
3. Treat perfectionism as a removable constraint: replace "this has to be perfect or else" with "done is better than perfect" when the goal is a draft.
4. When a person is the bottleneck, offer to remove their obstacles in person rather than escalating demands.
5. Remove obstacles strictly one at a time, primary first; re-identify the constraint after each removal.
