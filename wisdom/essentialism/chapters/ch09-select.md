# Chapter 9: Select — The Power of Extreme Criteria

## Core Idea
Replace broad, implicit criteria ("could be useful someday," "someone asked") with narrow, explicit, extreme criteria: "If the answer isn't a definite yes, then it should be a no." Selectivity is how exploration converges on the vital few.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 90 Percent Rule**: for any decision, identify the single most important criterion, score each option 0–100 against it; anything below 90 gets automatically changed to 0 and rejected.
  - When to use: any decision or dilemma, especially the agonizing in-between cases (the 7s and 8s on a 1–10 scale).
  - How: (1) name the one most important criterion; (2) score 0–100; (3) <90 → rewrite as 0, reject; (4) never deliberately keep a 60 or 70 — "think about how you'd feel if you scored a 65 on some test."
  - Why it works: numerical scoring forces conscious, logical decisions instead of impulsive or emotional ones, and kills indecision over near-misses. Failure mode / trade-off it forces: you will turn down genuinely good options on faith that better ones come — sometimes they don't; the point is that *you* choose which perfect option to wait for, by design rather than default.
- **HELL YEAH! or No** (Derek Sivers): total conviction → yes; anything less → no. Twitter-leader phrasing: "If the answer isn't a definite yes then it should be a no." Vitsoe's phrasing: "If it isn't a clear yes, then it's a clear no."
- **Minimum + Extreme Criteria screen** (for inbound opportunities): a written three-step filter.
  - When to use: opportunities that come *to* you — unexpected offers, easy cash, discounted deals — where fear of missing out distorts judgment.
  - How: (1) write down the opportunity; (2) write three *minimum criteria* it must pass to even be considered; (3) write three *ideal/extreme criteria*; fail any minimum → no; pass minimums but fail two of three extreme criteria → still no.
- **The advanced search (best slice of pizza in Brooklyn)**: search narrow, not broad — instead of "a good career opportunity," ask three questions: "What am I deeply passionate about?", "What taps my talent?", "What meets a significant need in the world?" Few results is the point; you want the one highest point of contribution, not many good options.
- **The killer question (ownership-bias breaker)**: for possessions, "If I didn't already own this, how much would I spend to buy it?"; for activities, "If I didn't have this opportunity, what would I be willing to do to acquire it?"

## Key Concepts
- **Extreme criteria**: deliberately over-strict selection thresholds that trade good options for the chance at perfect ones.
- **Implicit criteria**: the Nonessentialist's unspoken defaults — "if my manager asks, I do it"; "if others are doing it, I should be" — which social media amplifies.
- **Decision by design vs. by default**: selective criteria mean you choose; broad criteria mean other people or the universe choose for you.
- **Endowment effect (unnamed but used)**: we value what we already own above its worth, making elimination hard — hence the killer question.
- **Saying yes by default**: every failure to say no to a nonessential is an implicit yes.
- **The essential question of Part Three's transition**: not "what should I say yes to?" but "What will I say no to?" — the question that uncovers true priorities.

## Mental Models
- Apply the closet test to commitments: "is there a chance I'll use this someday?" clutters; "do I absolutely love this?" clears space for better.
- Treat explicit criteria as an empowerment device: once written, the most junior person can challenge the most senior ("Should we be taking on this account, given the criteria we have?").
- When an easy reward appears, model the hidden cost: saying yes now risks having to say no to a more meaningful opportunity later.

## Anti-patterns
- **Criteria drift**: an executive team started with three project criteria and decayed to one — "a customer asked." Result: overwork, collapsed morale (no project justified itself), and a commoditized market position; recovery required cutting "the 70 and 80 percents."
- **Accepting opportunities because they're easy to get**, not because they fit — FOMO-driven yes.
- **Broad search queries for life decisions**: "good restaurant in New York City" returns noise; so does "good career opportunity."

## Worked Example
Vitsoe (managing director Mark Adams; sells exactly one product, Dieter Rams's 606 Universal Shelving System) hiring an installer. Process: phone-first interview (strips visual cues, tests organization), multiple interviews, then a full trial day with the team, then a questionnaire to the whole team asking "Would he or she *love* working here?" and "Would *we* love having him or her work with us?" — no offer or commitment implied at that stage. The candidate installed flawlessly all day, but at pack-up he threw his tools into the box and shut the lid. The team read it as carelessness incompatible with their culture; Mark agreed and declined him. A "minor infraction" outweighed a flawless day because the primary criterion is "Will this person be an absolutely natural fit?" — and it isn't gut whim: Vitsoe iterates correlates empirically (e.g., intense childhood Lego play predicts culture fit). They would rather be understaffed than hire fast and wrong.

## Key Takeaways
1. Score options 0–100 on the single most important criterion; below 90 becomes 0.
2. Write your criteria down and make them public — explicit criteria empower pushback and resist drift.
3. Screen inbound opportunities with three minimum + three extreme criteria; failing two extremes is a no.
4. Ask the killer question to defeat ownership bias before keeping anything by inertia.
5. Search narrow: passion ∩ talent ∩ significant need, like Enric Sala holding out for explorer-in-residence over good-but-adjacent posts.
6. Ask "What will I say no to?" — not "what do I say yes to?" — to find true priorities.
